# Enterprise messaging in 2026: what earns trust

The research behind the skill's structural review for `marketing` mode, compiled 9 November 2026. **Caveat:** the session that gathered it could not open the primary reports (its network blocked them), so every figure below comes from published summaries of those reports. Treat the figures as indicative, and check one against its source before quoting it in a post.

## Thesis

An enterprise reader decides within the first two lines whether a post is evidence or a template. They research with AI tools and then check what they find against people, demos and peers. They punish text that shows the signals of machine writing: generic setup, formula devices, an audience that sounds invented. A post earns trust by carrying information only its author has: what happened, what it replaced, the thing itself, a measured result, and what the author learned.

Read together, the last three suggest the penalty attaches to what looks machine-made (structure, genericness), not to whether AI was used. Hence the rule below: check structure, not word lists.

## Practitioner principles (opinion, recurring across sources)

- **Position against what the reader does today, not against rival vendors.** April Dunford: the competitive alternative is "whatever buyers did before you arrived", often a spreadsheet or a manual process.
- **Lead with value; do not confuse it with answering objections** (Dunford, 2025).
- **Describe what the reader gets, and prove it.** "Proof, not promises" (Forrester 2026); unproven "#1" claims are the most common homepage fault.
- **Add first-hand information nobody else has** (Animalz on information gain, Nov 2025; content agency).
- **Show something people can try or see** (the Show HN norm; Simon Willison publishes each small tool with how it was built).

## The structural review

Seven signals that make a post read as a template, whatever its vocabulary:

1. **Question hook**: the first line is a question (`What came before this order?`).
2. **Generic setup**: a sentence the reader already knows, placed before the point (`The purchase order is only one part of the chain.`).
3. **Invented audience**: a persona the author did not have in mind (`someone who isn't in the details all day`), especially for a tool built for oneself.
4. **Competitor positioning**: framing against other tools instead of against the workaround the author replaced.
5. **Noun stacks**: nouns piled into a label (`selected-change context`) instead of what the reader sees.
6. **Defensive disclaimer**: an ending that pre-empts objections (`It isn't the full audit history, and approvals are inferred.`).
7. **Repeated devices**: more than one `not X, but Y` contrast, or lists of three used for rhythm.

And seven things that make it evidence:

1. **The incident**: what happened that made the tool necessary.
2. **The workaround replaced**: what the author did before, in concrete steps.
3. **The artifact**: a screenshot or short clip, with sample data if the real data is private.
4. **One measured number**, with how it was measured; never a borrowed statistic.
5. **One thing learned**: a gotcha, an edge case, a design choice that went wrong.
6. **Limits as facts**: stated plainly, where they apply, not as a closing defence.
7. **A specific ask** if the author wants one: a question practitioners can answer from experience, not engagement bait.
